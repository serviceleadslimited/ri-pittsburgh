## Free contractor visit versus paid independent inspection

These get conflated constantly, so here's the actual difference.

A **free contractor inspection** is the practical first move for a leak, visible damage, a storm, or an old roof. Someone competent gets eyes on it, tells you what's wrong, and can quote the fix. The obvious caveat: they're in the repair business. A good one will still tell you a repair beats a replacement — plenty do, because a homeowner who trusts them calls again in four years. A bad one won't. Photographs are how you tell them apart.

A **paid independent inspection** buys you an opinion from someone with no repair to sell. That's worth real money when the roof's condition affects a home purchase, a sale, an estate, an HOA decision, or a disagreement with an insurer or a contractor. In those situations a $350 report can settle an argument that free inspections just extend.

Neither is automatically right. Ask what gets inspected, whether the roof gets walked, whether the attic is included, what you receive in writing, and whether the recommendation separates repair from replacement.

## What moves the price

### Size and complexity

A single-story roof with easy access is quick. A large roof with multiple planes, dormers, additions, skylights, and chimneys is not. Pittsburgh's older housing — and 47% of city homes predate 1940 — piles on complexity right where it matters: masonry chimneys, roof-to-wall transitions, and hand-built valleys.

### Height, pitch, and safety

Steep or tall means fall protection, sometimes a lift, and sometimes an honest "I can't safely walk this, here's what I could see from the ladder and by drone." That limitation should be written down, not glossed over.

### Material

Asphalt, slate, tile, metal, and low-slope membrane fail in different ways and need different footing. Slate in particular is a specialty — walking it wrong breaks tiles, so fewer contractors will take it on and those who do charge accordingly. Say what you've got when you call.

### Documentation and testing

A free contractor visit is not the same product as a report with labeled photographs, moisture readings, attic review, and measurements. If a buyer, seller, insurer, or HOA is expecting specific paperwork, say so before scheduling — it changes who you should be hiring.

## Questions to ask before booking

1. Is the inspection free, and what does it cover?
2. What gets inspected, and what won't be accessible?
3. Do I get photos and something in writing?
4. Will you tell me if a repair works instead of a replacement?
5. Am I obligated to hire you afterward?
6. Are you insured, and what Pennsylvania registration applies to your work?
7. For a claim: what can you document without promising coverage?

Clear answers to those seven beat any certificate on a website. Urgency and a replacement number produced before any evidence is a bad sign, every time.

## When free is enough — and when it isn't

Free covers most homeowners most of the time: leaks, storm concerns, visible damage, maintenance planning, and figuring out whether work is needed at all. It works when you're comfortable asking questions and willing to get a second opinion on anything expensive.

Pay when neutrality is the point. If someone else — a buyer, an insurer, an HOA — has to accept the assessment, an independent report is usually cheaper than the argument you'd otherwise have.
