A roof leak is a water-path problem, not always a stain-location problem. Water can enter at a chimney, valley, vent, skylight, or roof-to-wall transition, then travel before it reaches a ceiling or wall. ## Common signs of a roof leak

Call promptly when you notice:

- A new brown, yellow, or spreading ceiling mark.
- Dripping, damp insulation, or a musty attic after rain.
- Peeling paint, swollen drywall, or soft trim near an exterior wall.
- Missing or lifted shingles, damaged flashing, or debris after wind.
- Water in a garage, porch, dormer, or upper-floor room.
- A leak that appears only during wind-driven rain or snow melt.

Do not assume a dry ceiling means the roof is fixed. The roof may be leaking only under a particular wind direction, or the interior materials may still be drying from an earlier event.

## How leak tracing works

A careful visit starts with your observations: when the water appears, what weather triggers it, and whether any roof work or exterior changes happened recently. The contractor may then review the interior, attic, roof surface, flashing, penetrations, gutters, and drainage path. Safe access and the roofing material determine what can be inspected directly.

The contractor should explain what was observed and what remains uncertain. Common source areas include:

### Flashing and roof transitions

Chimneys, dormers, sidewalls, skylights, and plumbing vents create joints that must shed water repeatedly. Old sealant, lifted metal, missing counterflashing, or improper overlap can let wind-driven rain into the assembly.

### Valleys and roof edges

Valleys collect water from two roof planes, while eaves and rake edges take repeated exposure. Leaves, ice, and poorly installed membrane can create a path under the covering. Gutter overflow can also mimic a roof leak at the fascia or interior wall.

### Roof covering and penetrations

Cracked shingles, slipped tiles, exposed fasteners, failed boots, and storm impact can all create openings. A small defect may cause a large interior mark when insulation or drywall channels water.

### Condensation and non-roof sources

Not every ceiling stain is a roof failure. Plumbing, HVAC condensation, attic ventilation, and ice-dam melt can create similar symptoms. A useful inspection says when the evidence points somewhere other than the roof.

## Pittsburgh weather and leak timing

Pittsburgh's rain, snow, freeze-thaw cycles, and ice can expose weaknesses that remain dry during a light shower. If the leak appears only during wind from a particular direction, tell the contractor. If it appears during snow melt rather than rain, note that too. Timing can narrow the likely water path.

After a storm, take exterior photographs from the ground if safe and keep the date. Do not climb onto a damaged or wet roof. If water is near electrical fixtures, treat it as an interior safety issue first and contact the appropriate professional.

## Repair, temporary protection, or replacement?

The right response depends on the source and the roof's remaining condition. A failed boot or small flashing defect may be a targeted repair. Widespread deterioration, damaged decking, repeated failed patches, or a roof near the end of its useful life may justify a broader plan. Ask for the evidence behind the recommendation and whether temporary protection is needed before permanent work.

For an insurance claim, keep photographs, invoices, inspection reports, and dates. A contractor can document observed storm damage, but the insurer decides coverage under your policy. Do not let a sales promise substitute for a written scope.
