Here's the thing about roof leaks: the water almost never comes in where you see it. It enters at a chimney or a vent boot or a sidewall, runs along a rafter, soaks the insulation, and shows itself on a ceiling somewhere else entirely. Which is why "stain over the back bedroom, patch above the back bedroom" fails so often. A Pittsburgh roof leak inspection finds the actual entry point and tells you honestly whether this is a repair or the start of a replacement conversation.

## How leak tracing actually works

It starts with your account, not the roof. When does the water appear? What weather brings it? Has anything changed up there recently — new gutters, a satellite dish, a chimney repointed, a solar install? Half of leak diagnosis is knowing which questions the house is answering.

From there the contractor works interior to exterior: the stain, the attic above it if it's accessible, then the roof surface, flashing, penetrations, and gutters. What can be reached safely depends on pitch, material, and weather. A good inspector will tell you what they couldn't get to and how that limits their conclusion. That admission is a sign of competence, not a hedge.

The usual suspects, roughly in order:

### Flashing and roof transitions

Chimneys, dormers, sidewalls, skylights, plumbing vents. Every one of these is a joint that has to shed water thousands of times a year, and Pittsburgh's freeze-thaw winters work those joints hard. Failed sealant, lifted metal, missing counterflashing, wrong overlap — this is where the majority of leaks live. On the older masonry chimneys common across the city and inner boroughs, deteriorated mortar can let water in above the flashing entirely, which no amount of roof work will fix.

### Valleys and roof edges

Valleys carry water from two planes at once, so they move more volume than anything else on the roof. Leaves pack in, ice dams form, and water backs up under the covering. Eaves and rake edges take constant exposure. And gutter overflow at the fascia produces an interior stain that mimics a roof leak so well that homeowners have paid for new roofs over it.

### The covering and its penetrations

Cracked shingles, slipped slates, exposed fasteners, split vent boots — rubber boots around plumbing vents are a classic ten-to-fifteen-year failure and one of the cheapest fixes on the list. Small opening, big interior stain, once insulation and drywall get involved.

### Things that aren't the roof

Attic condensation from bad ventilation. A sweating HVAC line. Ice dam melt driven up under the shingles. Plumbing in the wall. All of these produce ceiling stains, and the honest inspection is the one that says so and saves you a roofing invoice you didn't need.

## Pittsburgh weather and when leaks show up

Timing narrows the search considerably. A leak that only appears when rain comes hard from the west points at a specific wall or chimney face. One that shows during snow melt but not during rain usually means an ice dam, which is a ventilation and insulation problem as much as a roofing one. One that started after a windstorm suggests something got lifted.

So write it down. Date, weather, which room. It's genuinely more useful to a good roofer than another photo of the ceiling.

After a storm, take ground-level photos while it's safe and note the date. Do not get on a wet or damaged roof. And if water is near electrical fixtures, treat that as an electrical problem first.

## Repair, tarp, or replace?

Depends entirely on the source and what's left of the roof. A failed vent boot or a bad section of step flashing is a targeted repair — a few hundred dollars, not a project. Widespread deterioration, soft decking, patches stacked on patches, or a roof at the end of its service life is a different conversation.

Push for the evidence behind whatever they recommend. "It's shot" is not evidence. Photos of soft decking are. And if temporary protection is needed while you decide, get the price and the limits of it in writing separately from the permanent work.

For an insurance claim, keep everything: photos, invoices, the inspection report, dates. A contractor can document what they observed. Only the carrier decides what's covered, and any roofer who promises you an approved claim is telling you something they cannot know.
