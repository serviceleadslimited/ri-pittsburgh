## Free contractor inspection versus paid independent inspection

A free contractor inspection is often the practical first step when you have a leak, visible damage, or an aging roof. The contractor is evaluating the roof so they can recommend and price work. The visit may include photographs and a written estimate, but the contractor also has an interest in getting the repair or replacement job.

A paid independent inspection is different. You are paying for an opinion from someone whose business is the assessment or whose role is intended to be neutral. That can make sense when a roof condition affects a home purchase, sale, estate, HOA decision, or disagreement between a homeowner and a contractor.

Neither choice is automatically the right one. Ask what the person will inspect, whether the roof will be accessed when safe, whether attic or interior evidence is included, what you receive in writing, and whether the recommendation distinguishes repair from replacement.

## What moves the price

### Roof size and complexity

A small, single-story roof with easy access takes less time than a large roof with multiple planes, dormers, additions, skylights, chimneys, or steep sections. Pittsburgh's older housing stock often adds complexity at roof-to-wall transitions and masonry details.

### Height, pitch, and safety

A high or steep roof may require additional safety equipment, a lift, or a limited visual inspection rather than a walkable assessment. Unsafe access should be documented honestly rather than treated as a full inspection.

### Material and condition

Shingles, tile, metal, membrane, and slate have different failure patterns and access requirements. Moss, brittle material, prior repairs, and storm debris can increase the time needed to understand the roof without creating additional damage.

### Report and testing requirements

A basic contractor estimate is not the same product as a detailed written report with labeled photographs, moisture testing, attic review, measurements, or insurance documentation. Tell the inspector what the report must accomplish before accepting a price.

## Questions to ask before booking

Use these questions to compare offers in plain English:

1. Is the inspection free, and why is it free?
2. What areas will be inspected, and what will not be accessible?
3. Will I receive photos and a written scope?
4. Will you identify repair options if replacement is not necessary?
5. Is there any obligation to hire you afterward?
6. Are you insured, and what Pennsylvania registration information applies to the work you perform?
7. If this is for an insurance claim, what can you document without promising coverage?

A contractor who answers these questions clearly is easier to evaluate than one who relies on urgency, a generic certificate, or a replacement number before showing the evidence.

## When the free inspection is useful

Free contractor inspections are useful for leaks, storm concerns, visible damage, maintenance planning, and deciding whether a repair estimate is warranted. They are especially practical when you need a local response quickly and are comfortable comparing the recommendation with another opinion.

A paid independent inspection is more useful when neutrality is the point. If the seller, buyer, insurer, or HOA needs an assessment from someone without a direct repair incentive, paying for that independence can be cheaper than arguing about a replacement recommendation later.
