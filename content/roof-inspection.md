A Pittsburgh roof inspection should answer a simple question: **what condition is the roof actually in, and what should happen next?** That answer is more useful than a generic replacement pitch or a quick look from the ground.

## What the inspection covers

The contractor starts with the roof covering and follows the water-shedding system through the places most likely to fail. Depending on the property, that can include:

### Roof surface and materials

The inspection looks for missing or slipped shingles, cracked tiles, blistering, exposed underlayment, granule loss, soft spots, failed seams, and material that has reached the end of its useful service life. Surface age matters, but age alone is not a diagnosis. A well-maintained roof may remain serviceable, while a newer roof can have a flashing or installation failure.

### Flashing and penetrations

Chimneys, skylights, vent boots, valleys, dormers, wall transitions, and roof edges interrupt the roof plane. They are common leak paths because sealants, metal, and membranes move differently over time. The inspector should identify the location and describe whether the issue appears repairable or more extensive.

### Drainage and ventilation

Gutters and downspouts need to move water away from the roof and foundation. Debris, blocked outlets, poorly directed downspouts, and standing water can create repeat problems. Where accessible, the inspector can also note attic ventilation, condensation, and insulation conditions that affect moisture and roof life.

### Storm and seasonal damage

Wind, hail, heavy rain, snow, ice, and falling limbs can leave obvious damage or small failures that become leaks later. Photos and a dated written record are especially valuable after a storm, when several companies may be making urgent recommendations at once.

## Pittsburgh-specific inspection concerns

The Pittsburgh region sees wet seasons, freeze-thaw movement, snow and ice, tree cover, and older housing stock. Those conditions make chimney flashing, valley drainage, roof-to-wall transitions, and attic moisture worth explicit attention. Moss or staining may be cosmetic, but it can also conceal deterioration or keep roofing damp. The right answer depends on the material and the underlying condition, not on the color alone.

If you have an older home, tell the inspector about previous repairs, additions, skylights, solar equipment, and any room where staining has appeared. A leak can enter in one location and travel along framing before it becomes visible several feet away.

## Free contractor inspection or independent report?

A free contractor inspection is common when the contractor is estimating a repair or replacement. It can be a practical first step, especially when the visit includes photos and a written scope. An independent inspection may be worth paying for when you need a neutral opinion for a purchase, sale, insurance disagreement, or dispute with a previous contractor.

The important distinction is not the price by itself. Ask what the inspector will physically examine, whether the roof will be walked when safe, whether attic or interior evidence is included, what documentation you receive, and whether the recommendation includes a repair option. If a report says replacement is necessary, ask what specific observed conditions make repair unreasonable.

## Before the appointment

Gather any prior invoices, inspection reports, insurance correspondence, and photos of the issue. Mark the room where you noticed staining and note when it appeared. Do not attempt a roof walk in wet or unsafe conditions. If the roof cannot be accessed safely, the contractor should say what was and was not inspected rather than implying a full roof assessment.
